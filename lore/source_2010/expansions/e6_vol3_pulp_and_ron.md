### i. the boy from pasadena — the ward

*Sits after p.209 — follows `vol3_ch4_the_coast`, precedes `vol3_ch5_non_stop_flights` (play order: before the flight to Houston). Scene id `vol3_ch5_pulp_the_ward`.*

He opens his eyes and the light is already there, and it does not stop. It comes from long white tubes laid end to end down a ceiling that runs on farther than any ceiling ought to run, and Jack Perkins lies beneath it on a narrow cot railed in steel and cannot shut his eyes against it.

Somebody has put a salve on them. He can feel it there, cool and thick over the whites, and every blink he orders goes out from the brain and is turned back at the lid like a man at a guarded door.

He is not alone. He turns his head the one inch it will turn and sees the next cot and the man on it, and past that man another cot and another sleeper, a whole long row of them laid out like loaves on a baker's rack. Each one is tethered to the wall behind by bundles of cable and clear rubber hose.

The hoses run with liquors, red and gold and clear as creek water. The cables run to boxes of black glass where a green line climbs and falls, climbs and falls, is drawn across the glass and erased and drawn again. Every box keeps its own time. Every box chirps. A hundred crickets out of step in a summer with no night in it.

So this is the inside of the machine. He had expected gears. He had expected the hot green throat of a dynamo, the smell of ozone and burnt cork. Instead it is clean, cleaner than any laboratory he was ever allowed to stand in, cleaner than his mother's kitchen on a Sunday, and it smells of alcohol and of something under the alcohol that the alcohol is there to hide.

> choice: *linger on the boxes.* / *lie still.*

> [linger on the boxes.] The green line in the nearest box stumbles, skips, finds itself again and goes on.

> [linger on the boxes.] Under the chirping, low along the floor, runs a hum, constant, the breath of some cold engine keeping the whole room cool.

A figure comes down the row. It walks upright on two legs. It is clad head to heel in a garment the color of pond water, a paper muzzle over its mouth, a second skin over its hands so thin the knuckles show through like stones under a stream.

It stops at each sleeper. It reads the green lines. It touches a hose and the hose drips faster. It makes a mark on a board it carries and moves on, and when it comes to Jack it bends low until its eyes are close to his eyes, and the eyes are human. Brown and tired and ringed dark with lost sleep. Somebody's aunt.

It speaks. The words have English bones but the meat on them is wrong. Jack hears pressure in it, and numbers. He hears it call him honey, which cannot be right.

He tries to tell it his name. He tries to tell it the year he set out from and the coordinates of the arroyo and the institute that will vouch for him. His mouth won't shape a word. His tongue lies in his jaw like an old dog asleep on a porch.

The figure lays its gloved hand flat on his chest, the way a man lays a hand on a horse to settle it, and keeps it there awhile. Then it goes on down the row.

Overhead, past the white tubes, past the ceiling, the great machines are still coming and going from the roof. He can hear the long low thunder of them. They go out over the ruin with their pink lights and find the ones lying still and carry them in. That much he understands.

Somewhere down the row a door sighs open and sighs shut, and through it, for the length of a breath, the voice. Angry. Pleading. Almost English.

Jack Perkins lies under the light with his eyes open and counts the crickets and waits for his body to come back to him.

### i. the boy from pasadena — the catwalk

*Sits after p.233 — follows `vol3_ch6_jane` (Andy goes under in the sleep lab), precedes `vol3_ch7_emulsion`. Scene id `vol3_ch8_pulp_the_catwalk`.*

His body comes back to him the way feeling comes back to a foot, in needles. First the fingers. Then the long bones of the arms. Then all at once the rest of him, heavy and stupid and his own.

He sits up. The hose pulls at the crook of his elbow and he finds the place where it goes into him, taped down under a clear film. He peels the tape back and draws the needle out of his own vein slow, the way his father taught him to draw a splinter, steady and without apology. Blood beads. He presses his thumb to it.

The box behind him screams. It does not chirp now. It holds one long note, and Jack swings his legs off the cot and stands, and the floor is cold through his socks, and he goes.

Nobody stops him. The figures in pond-water green are bent over other sleepers, other screaming boxes. One of them looks up at him and looks back down.

The corridor runs on. It turns and runs on again. It is lined with doors and every door is shut and every door has a number and a little window of wired glass, and behind the glass the same white light, the same green lines. He walks a long time. The air is cold, scrubbed and blown down through vents, dry enough to split his lips.

Then the corridor leaves the ground. It becomes a tube of glass slung out over nothing, a catwalk between one tower and the next, and Jack stops in the middle of it with his hand on the rail and looks out on the ruin.

> choice: *look down.* / *look out.*

> [look down.] Far below, along a road, something moves in a long bright chain, red lights going one way and white lights the other.

> [look down.] On the near roof a vent breathes steam into the dark, and nobody is warming themselves at it.

It is night. The city goes on to the edge of seeing in every direction, and it is lit. Towers of lit windows. Roads of light poured through the dark and running with lights. Where he had seen fires there are lamps on poles, a thousand thousand of them, laid over the land like a quilt, and between the towers the machines go about their business, blinking red and white, lowering themselves gently onto the roofs.

Nothing is burning. He looks for the charred foundations and the cables hung at odd angles and the children crouched at their fires, and from up here he can find none of it. Only the light, and the business as usual of it, the end of the world going on without him.

He crosses into the next tower. There is a room of benches, and a box on a pole playing pictures to nobody, and a low table heaped with magazines whose covers show the same two towers, standing, then burning, then gone.

The children are here. They sleep on the benches in their coats with their shoes still on, grown and half grown. A woman sits upright with her eyes shut and her hands folded in her lap, waiting the way the old wait in train stations. A girl rubs at her eyes as if they hurt her and will not stop rubbing.

In this light they are not monsters. They are tired. Jack walks among them careful, the way you walk through a house where everyone has finally gone to sleep.

Down the hall a door sighs open, and the voice comes through it, and this time Jack Perkins is near enough to hear it break.

### i. the boy from pasadena — the room with birds

*Sits after p.250 — follows `vol3_ch8_passengers_c` (the ambulance, the noise), precedes `vol3_ch9_backward_jaunt`. Scene id `vol3_ch11_pulp_the_room_with_birds`.*

The room at the end is larger than the others and lit low. One whole wall is a window, and through the window Jack sees a lake in autumn, the water flat and copper, the reeds gone to rust, and on it wild ducks rising and settling, rising and settling, never all of them in the air at once.

It takes him a long moment to see that the window hangs from the ceiling on an arm of steel, and the lake is inside it, and it goes on with or without anybody watching. After a while the ducks change, and the lake changes, and it is a different lake in the same fall.

The voice comes from the bed.

Four of them hold the man down. They have him at the wrists and the shoulders and the shins, and he fights them with what he has, which is not much, and he spends all of it. He is trying to rise. He is trying to get up out of the bed and go somewhere, and the voice comes out of him angry and desperate and pleading, the words gone out of it and only the shape left.

They buckle straps across him. One of them puts a needle into the hose, and the fight goes out of him by degrees, like a fire banked for the night.

Two women in the corner, close to him but not around him. The older has her hand over her mouth. The younger stands and goes, fast, out past Jack in the doorway without once seeing him.

On the floor by the wall vent, where the cold air comes out humming, a boy too big for any bed lies curled in his own warmth. Bearded, his shoes falling apart on his feet. He has slept through all of it. He sleeps as if the hum were a lullaby somebody is singing just for him.

Jack goes to the bed.

The man is old, older than any man Jack has ever stood beside. His skin has gone the yellow of old piano keys and it is mapped with bruises. His gums are bleeding. His chest is bound in gauze. His eyes are too big in their sockets and roll there and will not settle.

Then they settle. They find Jack. They hold.

Jack Perkins's father was forty-four years old the night Jack sealed himself into the egg. He had dark hair and a hard jaw and a pipe he never lit, only chewed, while he graded proofs at the kitchen table. Jack carries a photograph of him in his billfold, gone soft at the corners.

The man in the bed opens his mouth. The voice that comes out now is quiet, its anger used up, and it says one word Jack can make out, and the word is his name.

He knows the voice. He has known it longer than he has known anything. He knew it before he knew it was a voice.

He looks down at the hand strapped on the blanket. The square nails. The knuckles knotted up. The little white scar across the back of the thumb from the summer the hatchet slipped at the lake cabin. Of all of it, only the hand.

> choice: *say it back to him.* / *take the hand.*

> [say it back to him.] Father, Jack says, and it comes out of him in a boy's voice, high and cracked, the voice of a child who has stayed out too long after dark.

Jack takes it in both of his. It is warm, and it grips back, weak, and holds.

Behind him on the hanging glass the ducks rise off the copper water, and settle, and rise.

### i. the boy from pasadena — earthman

*Sits after p.287 — follows `vol3_ch13_sins_of_our_fathers` ("He is earth man."), precedes `vol3_ch14_last_of_the_oil_men`. Scene id `vol3_ch17_pulp_earthman`.*

Jack sits by the bed through the night, or what passes for night in a place where the light never goes down. He has pulled a chair close. The man has stopped fighting. The straps lie loose across him now, unbuckled, nobody bothering.

Sometimes the man speaks. Not much, and slow, every word costing him. And Jack, who crossed half a century to find the future, learns its language the way he learned every language his father ever set in front of him. By drill. By being made to say it back.

What do you observe, the man asks him.

Jack observes. He says it aloud the way he was taught, from the general to the particular. The air is cold and dry and moving. The light is constant and its source is overhead. There are machines on the wall measuring the subject's heart, and the subject's heart is slowing.

There is a woman at the window who has not slept. There is a boy on the floor by the vent who has woken, and is sitting up now, and has taken the subject's other hand.

Good, the man says. And what do you conclude.

Jack cannot conclude. He opens his mouth and nothing is there.

Men come in the dark green and confer with the woman at the window, and she tells them to give him more. They ask if she understands what that will do. She tells them she breathes for people for a living. She knows what comes next. She does not get up from where she sits.

The man looks at Jack. Then at the boy on the other side of the bed. Then back at Jack. His eyes go between the two of them, slower each time, as if he were checking a sum.

Go on home, he says. Both of you. Go on.

Then the eyes go. Jack watches it happen. The light leaves them the way light leaves a lake when a cloud comes over, all at once and everywhere, until what is left is glass. Across the bed the big boy holds the other hand and weeps and does not try to be quiet about it.

> choice: *stay with the hand.* / *go to the window.*

> [stay with the hand.] It cools under his fingers by slow degrees, like a stone moved into the shade.

Outside, the great machines have stopped. Jack goes to the true window, past the lake still flickering with its birds on the hanging glass, and draws the curtain back, and the pink is going out of the sky. It is morning. Only morning.

On the roof below, a machine sits with its long blades drooping and its lights gone dark, and two of the green-clad figures sit on its runner in the cold, sharing one cigarette between them, passing it back and forth.

They were never hunting. All night they went out over the ruin with their lights and their sirens, and found the ones lying still, and carried them in, as many as they could reach. Plain as a proof.

In the room of benches the girl with the sore eyes has come back. She holds a yellow card. Somebody asks if she wants to go in and see him, and she says no, and Jack, passing, understands that too.

He walks out of the tower. Nobody stops him. The children of the ruin are getting up off the benches and the floors and going out into the morning with their coats over their arms, blinking. In daylight they are only children, grown and half grown. He was wrong about them. He was wrong about nearly everything.

The egg is where he left it, under the fallen street sign, which is only a street sign blown down in some storm with its cables still hanging. Dew stands on the hull. A bird has been at it.

He seals himself inside. He sits a long while with his hand over the button. He thinks of his father at the kitchen table chewing a cold pipe. He thinks of the old man with the bruised hands asking what he observed. He cannot make the two of them into one man. He cannot make them into two.

He pushes the button.

He gestates in the time between times and is born again, thusly, onto a cement floor, in a bare room, with ugly light creeping in under the door. Footsteps outside, drawing nearer. He lies still and waits to be found.

### ii. dreams in the key of godhead — ron takes notes

*Sits after p.291 — follows `vol3_ch14_last_of_the_oil_men`, precedes `vol3_finale`. First person, Andy, in the register of "notes from the underground". Ron speaks only in questions.. Scene id `vol3_ch19_ron_takes_notes`.*

Ron catches me on the stairs after supper and asks if I have an hour. He asks it like he already knows the answer.

His room is the way Betty said it would be. Books on the floor in drifts, dust hanging in the lamplight, nowhere for a body to stand. He clears a place on the bed for me with his forearm. He sits at the desk half turned away. The typewriter is black and the size of an engine block, and he rolls a fresh sheet into it and squares it and waits.

> choice: *look over his shoulder.* / *sit where he cleared.*

> [look over his shoulder.] The page is blank except for a title typed at the top, centered, and underlined twice.

The springs give under me and books slide off the far edge of the bed one after another, like men stepping off a curb.

**RON** Where were you, the day before Jack found you on his floor?

I tell him Houston. I tell him a hospital. He doesn't ask what Houston is like now, or what a hospital is like. He asks something else.

**RON** What did the air smell like?

**ANDY** Cold. Rubbing alcohol. And something under the alcohol.

He types. The keys go off like rifle fire down a canyon, a burst, a pause, a burst. The bell rings at the end of the line and he throws the carriage back without looking at it.

**RON** And the light?

**ANDY** There were screens over every bed. A green line on each one, going up and down. Little lights all over the machines, blinking. Like eyes.

**RON** How many machines?

**ANDY** A whole floor of them. The whole top floor.

**RON** Did they fly?

I laugh, the first time in I don't know how long, and it hurts like a cough.

**ANDY** The helicopters did. They landed on the roof all night. Bringing people in.

He types a long time on that.

**RON** What color were their lights, coming down?

**ANDY** Red and white. When they're both going and it's hazy out, it comes through the blinds pink.

**RON** And the ones they carried in, were they awake?

**ANDY** Mostly not.

**RON** Who was in the room with him?

I tell him. My mother by the window. My sister, who had to leave for a shot so she could go to Brazil and study rocks the way he did. Me.

**RON** And where were you?

I tell him the floor. I tell him I slept next to the air conditioner because I did that when I was a kid, the hum of it. I tell him I slept through the worst part and woke up when it was already almost over. I haven't said that out loud to anybody. I didn't know it was in me until it was out and in the room with the dust.

Ron doesn't stop typing to look at me. I am grateful to him for that.

**RON** Did they have to hold him down?

**ANDY** He tried to get up out of the bed. The drugs had him confused. They strapped him down and he fought them.

**RON** Did he say anything?

**ANDY** Not words, by then. He was angry. He sounded like he was begging for something.

**RON** Begging for what?

> choice: *tell him.* / *say nothing.*

> [tell him.] **ANDY** To go home. I think he wanted to go home.

He types, and I lean and watch the line come up out of the machine over his shoulder, letter by letter, black and wet.

A voice came booming from a machine among machines, it spoke a language not quite English, almost understandable. It was angry, desperate, pleading.

**RON** What did your father do?

**ANDY** Geologist. He taught. He took kids out into the field and showed them how to read a road cut. Oil, for a while.

**RON** Did he make you study?

**ANDY** He handed me books. Science fiction, mostly. Old paperbacks with the covers falling off.

**RON** Were they any good?

**ANDY** No. I read every one of them.

He types that too, or something like it. The bell rings.

**RON** And outside the hospital that night, what was the world doing?

**ANDY** Nothing. That's the thing. Cars on the freeway. Planes coming in to land. Everybody going to work in the morning. The world ended on one floor and nobody else got the memo.

He finds the line he wants and I watch it go down on the paper: the end of the world just outside, perhaps going on business as usual.

**RON** Do you mind?

I tell him no. I tell him take it. Somebody ought to.

**RON** How does it end?

**ANDY** He dies, Ron.

**RON** And the boy?

I lie back on his bed among the books. The typewriter goes on, burst and pause and bell, steady as a machine breathing for somebody. Down the hall Betty is laughing at something Jack said. The window is open and the air comes in heavy with oranges.

Ron asks me one more question. I am most of the way asleep by then and I don't catch what it is, only the shape of it, the way it lifts at the end.
