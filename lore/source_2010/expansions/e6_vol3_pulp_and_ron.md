His eyes are open and they will not close. The light is there and it does not stop, long white tubes laid end to end down a ceiling running on farther than any ceiling has a right to run, buzzing, flickering at the edges, and Jack Perkins lies under it on a narrow cot railed in steel and can't look away, can't blink, can't do much of anything.

Some salve or ointment sits thick on his eyes, cool, a greasy film over everything, the lights smeared to halos. He tells his eyelids to close and they don't listen, his arms don't listen, his legs he can't find at all.

He is not alone. He turns his head the inch it will turn and there is another cot and a man on it, and past that man another, and another, a long row of sleepers laid out like specimens in a museum drawer, each one tethered to the wall by bundles of cable and clear rubber hose.

The hoses run red and yellow and clear. The cables run to black glass boxes where a green line jumps and falls, jumps and falls, drawn across and wiped away and drawn again, every box keeping its own time, every box chirping, tiny lights blinking all over them like little eyes.

Crickets, a hundred crickets all out of step, a summer night with no night in it.

So this is the inside of the machine. He expected gears, dynamos, the hot green throat of a generator, ozone and burnt cork and sparks.

Instead it is clean, cleaner than any lab he was ever allowed into, cleaner than his mother's kitchen Sunday morning, and it smells of rubbing alcohol and of something under the alcohol the alcohol is meant to cover up.

> [linger on the boxes.] Under the chirping, along the floor, a hum, steady, the cold breath of some engine somewhere keeping the whole place cold.

It stops at each sleeper, reads the green lines, pinches a hose and the hose drips faster, makes marks on a board it carries, moves on. When it gets to Jack it bends down close, its eyes right up in his eyes, and the eyes are human. Brown and tired and dark underneath from no sleep. Somebody's aunt maybe.

It talks. The words have English in them but the English is wrong, bent, chopped up. He hears numbers in it, pressure, something about a line. He hears it call him honey and that can't be right.

He tries to tell it his name, the year he set out from, the coordinates of the arroyo, the institute that will vouch for him, his father's name. His mouth won't make a single word. His tongue just lies there, a dead thing in his jaw.

The figure puts its gloved hand flat on his chest and leaves it there awhile, then goes on down the row.

Overhead, past the tubes, past the ceiling, the great machines are still coming and going, he can feel the thunder of them through the steel of the cot, the roof taking their weight. Out over the ruin with their pink lights, picking up the ones lying still and bringing them here into the rows, for what purpose he can't begin to guess.

Somewhere down the row a door sighs open and sighs shut and through it for the length of a breath comes the voice. Angry, desperate, pleading. Not quite English. Almost.

Jack Perkins lies under the light with his eyes open and counts crickets and waits for his body to come back to him.

### i. the boy from pasadena — earthman

*Sits after p.287 — follows `vol3_ch13_sins_of_our_fathers` ("He is earth man."), precedes `vol3_ch14_last_of_the_oil_men`. Scene id `vol3_ch17_pulp_earthman`.*

Jack sits by the bed all night, or what passes for night in a place where the lights never go down. He has pulled a chair up close. The man has stopped fighting. The straps lie loose across him unbuckled, nobody bothering anymore.

Sometimes the man talks. Not much, and slow, every word costing him. And Jack learns the language of the future the way he learned every subject his father ever put in front of him. By drill. By saying it back.

What do you observe, the man asks.

Jack observes. He says it out loud the way he was taught, general to particular. The air is cold and dry and moving. The light is constant, its source overhead. There are machines on the wall measuring the subject's heart and the subject's heart is slowing.

There is a woman at the window who hasn't slept. There is a big kid on the floor by the vent who has woken up and is sitting up now and has taken the subject's other hand.

Good, the man says. And what do you conclude.

Jack can't conclude. He opens his mouth and nothing's there.

Men in dark green come in and confer with the woman at the window and she tells them give him more. They ask does she understand what that will do. She tells them she breathes for people for a living, she knows what comes next. She doesn't get up from where she sits.

The man looks at Jack a long time. Then he looks at the kid on the other side of the bed. Then back at Jack.

Go on home, he says. Go on.

Then his eyes go. Jack watches it happen, the light going out of them all at once and everywhere like a cloud over a lake until what's left is glass. Across the bed the big kid holds the other hand and cries and doesn't try to keep it quiet.

> [stay with the hand.] It cools under his fingers by slow degrees, a stone moved into shade.

### ii. dreams in the key of godhead — ron takes notes

The typewriter is black and big as an engine block. He rolls in a fresh sheet and squares it and waits.

> [look over his shoulder.] The page is blank except for the title at the top, centered, underlined twice.

The springs give under me and books slide off the far side of the bed, one, then another.

**RON** Where were you, the day before Jack found you on his floor?

I tell him Houston. I tell him a hospital. He doesn't ask what Houston is like now, or what a hospital is like. He asks something else.

**RON** What did the air smell like?

**ANDY** Cold. Rubbing alcohol. And something under the alcohol.

He types. Loud, a burst, a pause, a burst. The bell dings at the end of the line and he slaps the carriage back without looking.

**RON** And the light?

**ANDY** There were screens over every bed. A green line on each one, going up and down. Little lights all over the machines, blinking. Like eyes.

**RON** How many machines?

**ANDY** A whole floor of them. The whole top floor.

**RON** Did they fly?

I laugh, first time in I don't know how long, and it hurts like a cough.

**ANDY** The helicopters did. They landed on the roof all night. Bringing people in.

He types a long time on that.

**RON** What color were their lights, coming down?

**ANDY** Red and white. When they're both going and it's hazy out, it comes through the blinds pink.

**RON** And the ones they carried in, were they awake?

**ANDY** Mostly not.

**RON** Who was in the room with him?

I tell him. My mother by the window. My sister, who had to leave for a shot so she could go to Brazil and study rocks the way he did. Me.

**RON** And where were you?

I tell him the floor. I tell him I slept next to the air conditioner because I used to do that when I was a kid, the hum. I tell him I slept through the worst of it and woke up when it was almost over. I haven't told anybody that.

I didn't know I was going to say it until it was out in the room with the dust.

Ron doesn't stop typing to look at me. I am grateful to him for that.

**RON** Did they have to hold him down?

**ANDY** He tried to get up out of the bed. The drugs had him confused. They strapped him down and he fought them.

**RON** Did he say anything?

**ANDY** Not words, by then. He was angry. He sounded like he was begging for something.

**RON** Begging for what?

> choice: *tell him.* / *say nothing.*

> [tell him.] **ANDY** To go home. I think he wanted to go home.

He types. I don't look over his shoulder. I don't want to see what he does with it.

**RON** What did your father do?

**ANDY** Geologist. He taught. He took kids out into the field and showed them how to read a road cut. Oil, for a while.

**RON** Did he make you study?

**ANDY** He handed me books. Science fiction, mostly. Old paperbacks with the covers falling off.

**RON** Were they any good?

**ANDY** No. I read every one of them.

He types that too, or something like it. The bell rings.

**RON** And outside the hospital that night, what was the world doing?

**ANDY** Nothing. That's the thing. Cars on the freeway. Planes coming in to land. Everybody going to work in the morning. The world ended on one floor and nobody else got the memo.

He types a long time on that and doesn't ask me anything for a while.

**RON** Do you mind?

I tell him no. I tell him take it. Somebody ought to.

**RON** How does it end?

**ANDY** He dies, Ron.

**RON** And the boy?

I lie back on his bed with the books. The typewriter keeps going, burst and pause and bell. Down the hall Betty is laughing at something Jack said. The window is open and the air comes in heavy with oranges.

Ron asks me something else. I'm most of the way asleep by then and I don't catch it.
