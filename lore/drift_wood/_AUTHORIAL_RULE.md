# THE AUTHORIAL RULE · we don't see the author, but we feel him

The governing principle of vol 10, per the user (September 2026):

> The tragicomic nature of the comic is the author's delusions of
> grandeur and failing through life, and how it's reflected in his
> creation. We don't see the author, but we feel him. His hopes,
> dreams, fears and epiphanies, rendered through his work.

Everything in `drift_wood/` is read through this. It overrides any
earlier note in these files that stages Arthur directly.

---

## 1. The volume is the strip

The reader of vol 10 reads *Drift Wood* and then *ROFLCOPTER*, in
order, 1997–2027 — the way you'd read a complete *Calvin and
Hobbes* and come out knowing Watterson without a single page about
him. There are no VN scenes of Arthur at the framing shop, Arthur
at the wedding, Arthur at the drafting table. There are strips
about a character called **Wood** at the framing shop, at the
wedding, at the drafting table, drawn by a man we never see.

What the reader gets instead of the author:

- the strips, in run order, with their dates;
- the gaps (a week with no strip; nine months of reprints; a
  Sunday that is only weather);
- the margins (the signature, the mark beside it, the date, an
  occasional hand-lettered line);
- the **foreword and afterword** of the 2027 retrospective — the
  only prose in the volume, and the only place the author is seen.
  The foreword sets up the life without naming its hardships; the
  afterword tells them plainly and points back into the pages. See
  `foreword_afterword.md`.

That is the whole vocabulary. Between the bookends, the volume has
to say everything with the strip.

## 2. Wood is not Arthur

**Wood** is the strip's lead: the character Arthur draws when he
draws himself. **Arthur Finch** is the author: the name in the
margin, the hand, the man in the garage. The concept sheet already
knows this — it says *CHARACTER: WOOD*, not Arthur.

The distance between them is adjustable, and the adjustment is the
story:

- In 1997, Wood is Arthur with better lines. A brilliant exile
  among log trucks. The strip says so, at length.
- In 2000, Wood *boards the bus with Chloe.* The strip runs three
  days of Wood in Seattle. Then, without comment, Wood is back at
  the framing shop and the strip never mentions Seattle again. The
  reader knows what happened. The strip doesn't.
- In 2006, Wood is replaced for a week by a stick figure screaming
  acronyms. The reader is told nothing. When Wood returns he is
  drawn smaller.
- In 2014, Wood is the best man at the wedding. The dock Sunday
  (brief) is the truth leaking through: *"You're a coward, Arthur
  Finch."* — the strip's Chloe uses the author's name, once. That
  is the only time the name appears inside a panel in twenty-three
  years, and the volume should treat it as an event.
- In 2021, the private pages stop drawing Wood at all for months.
  When he comes back he is drawn from behind, or small, or as a
  reflection in a rain-streaked pane.

**Delusions of grandeur are rendered as strip content.** The
phantom graphic novel appears as a *COMING 2003* box in the corner
of a Sunday, then *COMING 2008*, then *2016*, in the same hand,
never remarked on. The scholarship Wood turned down becomes, in the
strip, a scholarship Wood was too good for. The forty thousand Digg
visitors become a strip about Wood ignoring fame. The reader feels
the author's hope and the author's lie in the same panel, and that
feeling is the volume's only mechanism.

## 3. Chloe, Gully, the dog

The strip's Chloe and the strip's Gully are the strip's. Whether
the real people are named Chloe Sterling and Todd Gulliver is
something the reader infers from the retrospective's apparatus, if
at all. The brief's biographies (`chloe_sterling.md`,
`todd_gulliver.md`) are *what the strip lets us infer*, written out
for the writer's convenience. None of it is on the page as fact.

The dog is the clearest case. No strip says Barnaby died. Three
silent Sundays run in November and December 2014 — the carry, the
empty bed, the box on the porch — and the reader knows, and the
strip never says. That is the model for every fact in the volume.

The **bird** on the concept sheet is now easy: it is a strip
choice. Wood's companion in the *ROFLCOPTER* pages is a rainbow
bird that has no business on this coast, and the reader who
remembers the three-legged pup asleep in the 2014 margin decides
for themselves what the bird is standing in for. The author
doesn't say. (Barnaby II may still be in the margin mark. The bird
is in the panels. Both, with no explanation, is the correct
amount.)

## 4. What each kind of thing looks like on the page

| the author's… | rendered as… |
|---|---|
| **hopes** | Wood succeeding at things Arthur didn't. The Seattle strips. The best man. A daily where a New York gallerist calls the frame shop by mistake and Wood plays it cool. |
| **dreams** | The phantom novel's *COMING* boxes. Genre shifts in the Sunday pages every four years (a noir Sunday in 2002; a travelogue Sunday in 2008; a magical-realist timber Sunday in 2016) that the strip presents as one-offs and the reader recognizes as the novel bleeding through. |
| **fears** | The 3:00 AM strips. The blank grid drawn as a strip. The suspended nib. The strip's Gully asking "For who?" |
| **failures** | Gaps. Reprints. The Sunday page shrinking. Wood's apartment getting smaller between eras without a moving-day strip. The one grease sack. |
| **epiphanies** | The silent Sunday landscapes. Nothing else. When Arthur understands something, the strip stops joking and paints weather. The reader learns to read the landscapes as the confessions. |
| **the burnout** | The last public Sunday: the 1997 cafeteria redrawn, the droplet falling, Wood walking into fog. Then no January file. |
| **the voyage** | Pages with no grid. Wood absent, then small. Places the strip never went. The town's whole record. The bird. |
| **peace** | The final page. Wood at the tide line, sketchbook under his arm, from a distance, the same size as the chimney and the mug. |

## 5. What this does to the files

- `_ARC_OUTLINE.md` — acts are **runs of strips**, not dramatized
  days. Every proposed scene id is a strip, a Sunday, a gap, or a
  piece of apparatus. Rewritten to match.
- `sample_strips.md` — the character in the panels is **Wood**;
  Arthur appears only in the margin. Samples added for the
  delusion-and-leak pattern.
- `arthur_finch.md` etc. — read as inference documents. A note at
  the head of each says so.
- The **prologue** editor's visit is no longer a VN scene. The
  retrospective frames the volume as a **foreword** and an
  **afterword** (`foreword_afterword.md`) and as the box numbers on
  the section dividers. The editor's one conversation with Wood is
  in the brief as a *strip* (Arc 5, Wednesday, Week 4) and stays
  one.
- The **player canon** sketch changes: the natural game is not
  "the player draws the strip" but **"the player is the editor"**
  — going through the boxes, choosing what the retrospective
  includes, never meeting the author either. See `_ARC_OUTLINE.md`.

## 6. The ending

Per the user: *if there is a happy ending, it's that his work is
published, but the specter of his own life is foreshadowed in its
pages.* The retrospective exists; the reader is holding it. The
afterword tells the life. And the reader, closing the book, can go
back and find the life drawn years before it happened — in a
corner box, a flat stroke, a droplet, a figure drawn from behind.
He drew all of it and couldn't read it. Whether the specter is
literal is reserved (`foreword_afterword.md`).

## 7. The test

Any proposed scene, strip, or page for vol 10 should pass:

1. Is Arthur on the page? If yes, it's wrong. Draw Wood, or draw
   the margin, or draw the gap.
2. Does the strip *say* the fact, or does the reader *infer* it?
   If it says it, cut the line and let the drawing carry it.
3. Is the hope and the lie in the same panel? If the strip is only
   sad, it's not tragicomic; if it's only funny, it's not this
   strip.
4. When the author understands something, did the strip go silent
   and paint weather?
