# FOREWORD & AFTERWORD · the bookends

Per the user (September 2026): *"There will be a foreword and
afterword that go into the author's life and hardships."* And:
*"If there is a happy ending, it's that his work is published, but
the specter of his own life is foreshadowed in its pages."*

These two pieces of prose are the only place in vol 10 where the
author is seen. Everything between them is the strip. The volume's
shape is therefore a **book**: the 2027 retrospective, read cover
to cover.

```
FOREWORD
  Drift Wood · 1997–2020     (the public strip, in runs)
  ROFLCOPTER · 2021–2027     (the private pages)
AFTERWORD
```

---

## What the foreword does

Read *before* the strips. It has to make the reader want to read
ten thousand strips by a man they've never heard of, and it has to
plant, without spoiling, the things the afterword will pay off.

- **Who he was, as the town knew him.** The golden boy, the school
  paper, Most Likely to Win a Pulitzer. The scholarship. The frame
  shop. The motel. "You've probably seen one thing he drew, and it
  was the helicopter."
- **What the strip is.** The cadences, briefly: every two weeks,
  then five a week, then six and a Sunday for sixteen years. The
  coastal papers. The Sundays. That it stopped in December 2020
  without a word, and that boxes 21 through 40 exist.
- **How to read it.** The foreword tells the reader, gently, that
  the character is called Wood and the author was called Arthur,
  and that the distance between them is the point. It tells them
  to watch the margin. It does not tell them what they'll find
  there.
- **What it withholds.** The hardships. The foreword says *there
  were hardships* and says the afterword will speak to them. It
  does not name them. The reader goes into the strips knowing a
  shape and not a fact — which is exactly the position the strip's
  own readers were in for thirty years.

Voice: warm, a little formal, a person who admires the work and is
being careful with the man. Length: short. Four or five pages of
the book.

## What the afterword does

Read *after* the last page. It goes into the life and the hardships
plainly — the things the strips rendered as Wood's small
triumphs, as gaps, as weather.

- **The life, told straight.** The scholarship turned down and what
  it actually cost. The father's illness and death. The rent. The
  years the strip paid $40 a month. The wedding he drove to on a
  doughnut spare. The dog. The second dog. The name he couldn't
  shed. The burnout. The six years in the garage drawing for no
  one.
- **The hardships the strip never showed.** Reserved in detail, but
  this is where they go: the things Wood was never allowed to
  have. What the afterword reveals should send the reader back to
  specific pages. (See the foreshadowing index below.)
- **The publication.** How the retrospective came to exist: the
  editor, the boxes, the decision. *His work is published.* This is
  the happy ending, and the afterword is allowed to say so.
- **The specter.** The afterword's last move is to point back into
  the pages: to say that what happened to him is *in there*, drawn
  years before it happened, in a corner box, a flat stroke, a
  droplet, a figure drawn from behind. The reader closes the book
  and knows the strips foreshadowed the life. That is the
  tragicomedy completing itself: he drew it all, and couldn't read
  it.

Voice: reserved. Whoever writes it loved him and is not going to
perform that. Length: longer than the foreword. Ten pages of the
book, with dates.

## Who writes them · reserved, with a lean

Options, each a different volume:

| foreword | afterword | what it does |
|---|---|---|
| the editor | the editor | Cleanest. One outside voice bracketing the work; the retrospective's own apparatus. The brief's editor, grown up over the project. |
| the editor | **Chloe** | The afterword by the one person who could deconstruct his pretenses before he finished speaking. Devastating and earned. Risk: it makes Chloe "real" in a way the strips never do. |
| Chloe | Todd | Two voices from inside the strip's own cast, which means the strip's Chloe and Gully turn out to have been people. The most works-within-works. |
| the editor | *no one* — a note that the afterword was to have been written by the author | Only if the specter is literal. See below. |

**Lean: the editor writes the foreword; Chloe writes the
afterword.** The foreword is a stranger's admiration; the afterword
is a friend's account. The reader has spent thirty years of strips
with the strip's Chloe and now hears the real one, once.

## Is the specter literal · reserved

*"The specter of his own life"* can be read two ways, and the
choice changes the afterword's tense:

1. **He is alive at publication.** The afterword tells the
   hardships; the specter is the shadow the life cast over the
   work — the reader now sees the pages as haunted by what they
   were compensating for. The final page (April 2027) is the last
   thing he drew *before the book*, and he is, as the brief has it,
   at peace.
2. **He is not.** The afterword is written after. The retrospective
   is posthumous or nearly; the "happy ending" is that the work
   outlives the hardships; the specter is literal, and the pages —
   the empty chair, the flat stroke, the walk into fog, the cedar
   branch going out to sea — foreshadowed it.

The brief's finale (a man at peace on a cedar log) is (1). The
user's phrasing leans toward (2) but doesn't commit. **Decide
before the afterword is drafted; nothing else in the volume needs
the answer yet.** If (2), the final page's signature — *Arthur
Finch, 1997–2027* — becomes the thing the whole volume was walking
toward, and the foreword must not give it away.

## The foreshadowing index · what the pages plant

The afterword points back into the strip. These are the plants the
writer should make on purpose so there's something to point at.
Each is a strip or a margin, never a statement.

| plant | where | what it foreshadows (the afterword's reading) |
|---|---|---|
| The *COMING 2003 / 2009 / 2017* corner boxes | a digest cover, June 2002; Sundays, June 2008 / 2016 | The novel that never came; the four-year cycle of the life. |
| The three Seattle dailies, then the framing shop | Sept 2000 | Every leaving he didn't do. |
| Wood drawn at two-thirds size for six weeks | Nov–Dec 2006 | What the name did to him. |
| The one grease sack | March 2005 | The father. |
| Nine months of re-inked 2001 reprints | 2010 | The death, never drawn. |
| The flat stroke where the gull goes | Nov 30, 2014 | The first time the margin went silent. |
| The ink droplet that won't fall / that falls | Nov 30, 2014 / Dec 27, 2020 | The hand refusing; the hand letting go. |
| The bowling-alley neon with the L out | "Lanes," 2009, and again in 2019 with more letters out | The town, and him, going dark a letter at a time. |
| The glow on the ridge in the landscapes | from 2009, larger in the private pages | Reserved. The one thing in the strip the afterword can't explain. (The saga's echo, if any.) |
| Wood drawn from behind, or as a reflection | the private pages | The author disappearing from his own work. |
| The bird | the private pages | Whatever the afterword says it was — a dog, a daughter, a leaving, a lie. Reserved. |
| The cedar branch floating out | the final page | The title, finally. Drift wood. |
